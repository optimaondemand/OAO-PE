Welcome, Sport Scientist in Training

For most of your life, movement has been instinct: you ran because you wanted to run. In this course we shift the lens. This year you become a sport scientist — someone who measures, predicts, and analyzes the body. You will collect data on your own performance, test hypotheses about training, and use evidence to design plans that make you faster, stronger, and more durable.

Lesson 1
Why We Measure Movement

Physical activity is any movement that uses your muscles and burns energy, but for a sport scientist the more useful question is how much, how hard, and how often. Health researchers agree that kids your age should accumulate at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity per day. That number is not a guess — it comes from decades of studies linking daily movement to stronger hearts, better mood, and sharper focus.

Why does this matter right now? Because middle school sits inside a major growth window. Bone density, heart size, and the neural wiring between brain and muscle are all being built right now. Your body treats movement as a signal: enough signal, and the hardware develops well; not enough, and the system underbuilds. The training you do this year literally shapes the body you'll carry into high school.

Sport Science

Your heart beats roughly 100,000 times each day, moving about 2,000 gallons of blood through a closed loop of vessels. During hard exercise, that rate can climb from a resting baseline of 60–90 beats per minute to over 180. The blood it pumps carries oxygen to your muscles, where it becomes the chemical fuel that powers movement. The efficiency of that whole delivery system is called your VO₂ max, and it is one of the most important numbers in sport science.

Lesson 3
How Movement Reshapes the Brain

Exercise is not just a body event. When you move, the brain releases chemicals called endorphins that elevate mood and reduce the perception of pain. Activity also boosts BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) — a protein that helps brain cells grow and form new connections. In short: movement is one of the strongest known signals for brain development at your age.

Kids who are active most days of the week tend to focus better in class, retain more of what they read, fall asleep faster, and recover from stress more quickly. None of that is opinion — it shows up in studies that track thousands of students over many years.

Did You Know?

Researchers have run brain scans on students immediately after exercise and found increased activity in the prefrontal cortex — the region you use for attention, planning, and decision-making. This is one reason a five-minute movement break can pull you out of a math slump. The effect is measurable for up to two hours after exercise.

Lesson 4
The Skills That Don't Show on a Stopwatch

PE also trains a different category of skill — sometimes called character skills or non-cognitive skills. These include communication, leadership, sportsmanship, and resilience. They cannot be measured with a stopwatch, but they predict success in sport, school, and work just as strongly as physical performance does.

Every game you join is a rep on these skills: cooperating with teammates, accepting an honest call against you, calling for the ball, refusing to quit after a setback. Sport scientists who study elite athletes consistently find that the mental game separates the good from the great.

Real-World Connection

Top performers across sports — Simone Biles, LeBron James, Lionel Messi — credit their mental training as much as their physical training. Professional teams now employ performance psychologists who work alongside strength coaches and nutritionists. The reps you put in during a 6th-grade game build the same neural pathways an elite athlete uses to handle a championship moment.

Measure Your Baseline
Coach's Lab

Baseline test — your resting heart rate. Sit quietly for two minutes. Then place two fingers on the side of your neck or the inside of your wrist. Count beats for 30 seconds and multiply by 2. Record that number. We'll come back to it throughout the year and watch it change as you train. Lower resting heart rate over time is one of the clearest signs your cardiovascular system is getting stronger.
