Lesson 1

What an Exercise Physiologist Actually Does

Exercise physiology is the science of how the body responds to physical stress — short-term during a workout, and long-term across weeks and years of training. Researchers in this field run controlled experiments: they measure a baseline, deliver a precise intervention (a workout, a supplement, a sleep schedule), then re-measure to see what changed. The goal is not opinion. The goal is evidence.

That same scientific lens is what separates a coach who guesses from one who knows. When a trainer says "this drill builds explosiveness," a junior physiologist asks: How would we know? What would we measure? Compared to what control? Over how many weeks? Those four questions are the foundation of every training decision you'll learn to make this year.

🧪Sport Science

The current evidence-based recommendation for kids 6–17 is at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity daily, plus muscle- and bone-strengthening activity on three days per week. That number comes from large studies tracking thousands of children for decades. But notice the precision: not "be active," but 60 minutes, moderate-to-vigorous, daily, with strength work 3×/week. That's the kind of dose-specific recommendation a physiologist looks for.

Do it

Build the Investigation

A trainer claims: "This drill builds explosiveness." You can't accept or reject that yet — you have to test it. Tap each of the four questions a physiologist asks to turn the claim into a real experiment.

You need an observable outcome — not a feeling. Pick a number that "explosiveness" should move: a vertical-jump height, a 10-meter sprint time, or a standing broad-jump distance.
Choose one measurable metric and record it the same way every time — e.g. vertical jump in centimeters, same wall, same shoes, three tries, best counts.
Improvement only means something against a comparison: the same athletes before vs. after, or a control group doing their normal routine while one group does the drill.
Set a time frame that matches the biology. Neural power gains show in 2–4 weeks; a fair test of a power drill runs at least 4–6 weeks of consistent training before you re-measure.

💡Did You Know?

The first measurable adaptation to a new training program is almost always neural, not muscular. In the first 2–4 weeks, you get stronger mostly because your nervous system learns to recruit more muscle fibers at once — not because the fibers themselves grow. Visible muscle growth (hypertrophy) takes 8–12 weeks of consistent training. Knowing this protects you from giving up after a month of "no visible change."

Lesson 3

Why the Brain Is Part of the Training

Every workout you do also changes your brain. Exercise releases endorphins and elevates BDNF — brain-derived neurotrophic factor — a protein that helps neurons grow and connect. Studies on adolescents show that students who exercise regularly score better on attention, memory, and executive-function tests than matched peers who don't.

But the evidence is not unlimited. The effect on academic grades is real but modest — roughly a 0.1–0.3 grade-point bump on average. The effect on mood is larger and more consistent. The effect on sleep quality is also robust. A junior physiologist treats movement as one input among many — important, but not magic.

📊Case Study

The "Hancock Park" attention study (multiple replications, 2014–2022): Researchers measured 6th–8th graders' performance on a sustained-attention task before and after 20 minutes of moderate exercise. Across more than a dozen studies, exercise produced an average 4–7% improvement in attention scores that lasted up to two hours. The effect was bigger in kids with lower baseline fitness. Lesson: a movement break before a hard mental task is supported by real evidence — but it doesn't replace sleep, hydration, or actually studying.

Lesson 4

The Skills That Don't Show on a Stopwatch

Performance is more than physical output. Sports researchers measure a parallel set of skills they call psychological readiness — communication, leadership, emotional regulation, persistence after failure. These are harder to test but predict long-term success in sport as strongly as physical metrics do, sometimes more.

The clearest example is grit research: studies tracking athletes across multiple seasons find that the ability to keep training through plateaus is a better predictor of who reaches the highest level than initial talent. Talent gets you noticed; consistency gets you somewhere.

🌎Real-World Connection

Most professional teams now employ performance psychologists alongside strength coaches and athletic trainers. The NBA, NFL, and most MLB teams have mental-performance specialists on staff. Olympic teams have done this since the 1980s. The job is not therapy — it's training arousal regulation, visualization, focus skills, and recovery from setbacks. Career paths include sport psychology, exercise physiology, athletic training, biomechanics, and nutrition science — all of them growing fields.
